Pinnacle

01 — Overview

An app that teaches, rewards, and connects

Pinnacle is an educational fitness app that pairs support with incentive. It's built around three ideas: a learning feature to build confidence, an incentive to keep people coming back, and a supportive community of both novices and trainers.

The moment

Mid-2020. With studios closed and time suddenly abundant, at-home fitness exploded — and a wave of total beginners started working out for the first time, mostly by following along to videos. Pinnacle was my response to that moment.

02 — The problem

Starting is easy. Staying is hard.

Almost everyone is motivated to exercise at some point — but the motivation to maintain a routine tends to fall flat. The constraints are partly practical (time) and partly mental: we lose the confidence to keep pushing, especially when we're unsure we're even doing it right.

How might we
"How might we help people maintain their fitness health?"

The solution I landed on: an app that teaches correct form so beginners stop googling "correct plank form" mid-workout, rewards consistency with points redeemable for coupons, and connects novices with trainers who share their own programs and build a following.

03 — Research

A hunch, then the proof

Secondary research. The signals were everywhere: fitness-equipment demand jumped 55% in a single mid-March week per the Digital Economy Index, major studios from Planet Fitness to Nike Training Club rushed out free programs, and SocialBlade showed fitness creators like Chloe Ting pulling 100M+ views in a month. People were exercising more than ever.

A public survey. I ran a short survey across my social platforms to understand why. Two insights cut through: people had picked it up partly out of pandemic boredom and newfound time, and they strongly preferred following along to video — being able to pause, replay, and watch a visual reference made all the difference.

In their words

"I knew there were videos, but I didn't start until quarantine — it's nice watching someone else do it on video, because you can pause and stop whenever."

04 — Who I designed for

Two sides of one community

Pinnacle is built for both fitness novices and fitness experts. A college student new to working out and a seasoned trainer should each find a purpose in the app — and ideally learn from each other when their paths cross. Personas spanning that range kept my decisions honest, like Mason Fox, who just wants to feel confident in his clothes.

  • "As a dad, I need a consistent, healthier lifestyle so I can keep up with my kids."
  • "As a high-school senior, I need to start working out so I can find the courage to ask Stacey on a date."
  • "As a fitness trainer, I need to mentor people toward healthier lives — which fulfills my own mission too."

05 — The solution

Three features, three jobs

01
Educate

Workout library

A Wikipedia-style reference paired with a video demo for every move — animated or real-life — so beginners can learn proper form and understand exactly what each exercise targets. It takes the fear and guesswork out of getting started.

02
Empower

Programs & recommendations

Users build their own workouts, or — if they don't know where to start — get recommended pre-made programs. This serves novices and gives trainers a way to share their programs and grow a following.

03
Motivate

Challenges & points

Quick daily challenges spark a little competitive fire, and finishing workouts — or having your shared program completed by others — earns points redeemable for coupons at favorite stores. A reason to come back, because the hardest step is starting.

07 — Testing & iteration

Where the first version was wrong

A/B testing. Five participants compared two layouts; four preferred seeing their progress first. They also flagged that it took too many clicks to get there — so I moved progress into the profile, a place people visit often anyway.

Usability testing. Five more participants walked the app over video calls — three pandemic-era beginners and two long-time gym-goers. What I heard:

  • The workout library and its in-depth tutorials were the clear favorite.
  • Consistent, welcoming branding made the app feel credible.
  • The stats card confused people — it wasn't clear you could tap to flip it.
  • My original vibrant red read as "danger," not the feeling you want after a workout.

That last point opened the most important change. My first iteration used a bold red background — and tested against green, it failed WCAG AA and AAA contrast outright, which would have excluded color-blind and low-vision users. I rebuilt on a neutral black ground and hit AAA across the board. I also moved from pure graphics to a hybrid of icons and real photos, and added a "resume your latest workout" shortcut on the home screen.

08 — Reflection

No one starts perfectly

Pinnacle taught me to articulate why one design beats its alternative, not just to trust my gut — and that iteration isn't a failure state, it's the work. It also surfaced a real tendency of mine toward vivid color, and pushed me to earn restraint through accessibility rather than aesthetics alone.

It's an early project, and it shows its age in places. But the instincts I leaned on here — research first, design for the edges, test honestly, iterate — are the same ones I carried into enterprise work at IBM.
